# Guide 2 · The Judgment Ladder

*A climbable set of questions that moves a student from "I saw a thing" to "I changed my practice."*

---

## The big idea

Clinical judgment isn't a switch you flip on at graduation — it's a staircase. The classic framework (Tanner's, taught with Benner's flavor) has four steps. Your job as an educator is to know **which rung a student is standing on**, and ask the question that belongs to the rung *just above* it. Ask too low and they're bored. Ask too high and they panic and retreat.

## The ladder

| Rung | Name | The student can… | Your questions sound like… |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Noticing** | Say what they observed, plainly | "What did you see?" · "What made you look twice?" |
| 2 | **Interpreting** | Say what it *might mean*, with options | "What are three explanations for that?" · "Which fits best, and why not the others?" |
| 3 | **Responding** | Say what they'd *do*, in order | "Walk me through your next hour." · "What's step one if you're wrong about this?" |
| 4 | **Reflecting** | Say what changed in their thinking | "What surprised you?" · "What will you check earlier next time?" |

Two bonus rungs educators quietly use:

- **Rung 0 — Data floor.** Before anything: "What were the actual numbers/words/sounds?" Writing down what they *actually* noticed (not what they now claim to have noticed) resets arguments fast.
- **Rung 5 — Transfer.** "Where else in the body, the hospital, or your life does this same pattern show up?" This is where a single case becomes a reusable mental model.

## How to run a ladder session (3 minutes per student)

1. Ask your **Rung 1** question and take notes. Do not correct anything yet.
2. Choose the rung one step above their weakest link — not three steps above.
3. Climb **one rung at a time**, no skipping. Each rung is worth doing slowly; the ladder isn't a quiz show.
4. Stop at Rung 4 even if the student stalls — a clean "I don't know, and here's what I'd check next time" *is* Rung 4.
5. End on a note: "That's the rung we call X. Feel what it's like to stand there."

## Ladder chains for five common scenes

Use these as-is. Each chain starts low and climbs.

### Chain 1 · The missed trend

Steady heart rate at 110 across four hours; student records it and moves on.

1. "Read me the four heart rate values, in order." *(Rung 1)*
2. "Draw the line in the air with your finger. Which way is it moving?" *(Rung 1 → 2)*
3. "Name two things that could make a body sit at 110 for four hours." *(Rung 2)*
4. "Which of those two would you rule out first, in the first five minutes?" *(Rung 3)*
5. "What single phrase would you add to your next report, just for this?" *(Rung 4 → 5)*

### Chain 2 · Panic at the wrong moment

Student freezes at a loud monitor alarm but shrugged at the quiet change in a patient's speech.

1. "What sounds were in your hallway today? List three." *(Rung 1)*
2. "Which of those three told you the most about a patient?" *(Rung 2)*
3. "Why do you think the loud one grabbed you and the quiet one didn't?" *(Rung 2, metacognition)*
4. "Design your own triage rule: what gets 60 seconds of you before anything else?" *(Rung 3)*
5. "How would you teach that rule to a brand-new CNA on your unit?" *(Rung 5)*

### Chain 3 · The over-confident caller

Student escalates immediately on a finding they misread.

1. "What exactly did you see that made you call?" *(Rung 1)*
2. "How many explanations did you consider before you called — name them, even after the fact." *(Rung 2)*
3. "Next time, what's the *cheapest* thing you'd check before you lift the phone?" *(Rung 3)*
4. "Was calling wrong in this case, or just early? What's the difference?" *(Rung 4)*

**Educator note:** never punish the call. Escalating early is a habit we want; we're refining the notch between "call now" and "gather one more datum, *then* you call."

### Chain 4 · The confident wrong answer

Student states a wrong conclusion fluently and defends it (this is the "debrief fuel" case).

1. "Say your conclusion again — slowly — then tell me the single fact it's standing on." *(Rung 0 → 1)*
2. "If that one fact turned out to be recorded wrong, would your conclusion survive? Say it out loud." *(Rung 2)*
3. "What're you going to double-check in your next assessment because of today?" *(Rung 3 → 4)*

**Educator note:** confident + wrong is the *most teachable* moment in clinical education. The confidence is a stress response, not arrogance. Honor it, then put one fact under it.

### Chain 5 · The family handoff miss

Family told the student something in the hallway; it never made it to the record or to you.

1. "What did the family say, in their words?" *(Rung 1 — insist on their words, not a translation)*
2. "What did you think it meant at the time?" *(Rung 2)*
3. "Where in your shift would that sentence have been written down?" *(Rung 3)*
4. "Design the doorway moment: first 30 seconds meeting a family, what do you ask?" *(Rung 5 — leads straight into [Guide 3](03-family-to-nurse-handoff-teaching.md))*

## Prompt cards (print these)

Cut these into card-sized strips; students draw one when discussion stalls.

- "Describe what you saw with zero interpretation words." *(Rung 1)*
- "Give me your top explanation — then argue *against* it for 30 seconds." *(Rung 2)*
- "You have three minutes and one phone call. What do you spend them on?" *(Rung 3)*
- "What would a brand-new nurse do differently than you did today?" *(Rung 4, self-aware)*
- "Which of your habits from today will still be with you in five years?" *(Rung 5)*

## Grading the ladder, lightly

You don't score every debrief, but when you do, use a three-point "rung reached" mark:

| Mark | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| R1 | Stayed at noticing: described events but no meaning drawn |
| R3 | Reached interpreting/responding: offered a plausible explanation and an action |
| R5 | Reached reflection/transfer: named what they'd change or where the pattern recurs |

Track each student's rung over a semester — the climb is the grade. When you're ready for a full observable rubric instead, use [Guide 4](04-handoff-rubric-builder.md).
