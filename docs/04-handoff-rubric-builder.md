# Guide 4 · Handoff Rubric Builder

*Build a fair, observable scoring rubric for any handoff skill — in about 20 minutes.*

---

## The big idea

Most handoff rubrics fail for one reason: the criteria aren't *observable*. "Demonstrates good communication" can't be scored; "states their top concern in one sentence" can. This guide walks you through building a criteria set that two different graders would score the same.

**The 20-minute construction loop:**

1. Pick **one scene** (a shift handoff to the nurse, a family doorway conversation, a report-off call).
2. Watch or recall **one real-but-fictionalized example** of it going well and one going poorly.
3. Pull four to six criteria out of the *difference* between those two runs, and write them so a stranger could watch for them.

## Rule set

Follow these five rules strictly. Every bad rubric in the wild breaks at least one of them.

| Rule | Why |
| --- | --- |
| **4–6 criteria, never more** | Attention is finite; beyond six you're grading paperwork, not practice |
| **Only observable verbs** | *"states," "names," "asks," "writes"* — never *"understands," "demonstrates professionalism"* |
| **Each criterion stands alone** | If you can cram two checks into one sentence, it's two criteria |
| **Whole-number anchors** | 0 (absent), 1 (partial), 2 (present and complete) |
| **Same weight unless you can hear yourself defying it** | When you must weight (safety > charm), say why inside the rubric itself |

## Anchor language in one table

For every criterion, write all three levels. If you can't fill in "What you'd hear at level 1," the criterion is too vague.

| Score | You observe… |
| --- | --- |
| **0** | Missing entirely, or hearsay ("I told her" without evidence) |
| **1** | Present but fuzzy: vague words ("soon," "a lot"), no anchor times, no names for concerns |
| **2** | Present and complete: crisp facts, anchors when they matter, one top concern stated plainly |

## Worked example: the shift-to-shift nurse handoff

Here's a finished, tested rubric. Score Sample's fictional handoff with it before you build your own.

**Scene:** End of shift. Student hands off their six-bed assignment to the oncoming nurse, in 2–3 minutes, at the bedside or board (fictional patients only, ever).

| # | Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Names each patient, room, and one-line reason for admission** | Missing or guessing from memory | Some rooms/patients mismatched | All three match, no pauses |
| 2 | **States each patient's top current concern in one sentence** | Skipped | Wandered ("they're, uh, stable-ish") | Plain sentence per patient |
| 3 | **Anchors time-sensitive items to clock times or "sick/being watched" status** | Vague ("later," "soon") | Partial anchoring | Every time-bound task anchored |
| 4 | **Flags one expected problem and the plan for it** | None or unexplained | The plan is only a code phrase ("we'll see how it goes") | Problem + concrete next action |
| 5 | **Invites the receiver: asks what the oncoming nurse wants to double-check** | Doesn't ask | Asks, but answers for them ("nothing you'd need, right?") | Real question, waits for a real answer |
| 6 | **Hands off the record cleanly: says where the latest numbers and (fictional) family notes live** | Doesn't mention it | Points vaguely | Names the exact chart location aloud |

*(Note for the printable worksheet: this six-row table is the "starter rubric" — reuse rows freely.)*

**Score Sample's practice handoff:** a student says, "Room 304, the Sorta Confused lady — she's fine, meds are okay, if she gets upset just redirect. Next room… Room 310…" Score honestly:

- Criterion 1 ✓ 1
- Criterion 2 ✗ 1 (never stated; "fine" is a mood, not a sentence)
- Criterion 3 ✗ 0 (no clock anchors anywhere, no escalation status)
- Criterion 4 ✗ 1 (one problem named — confusion — but the plan is a vibe)
- Criterion 5 ✓ 0 (never invited the receiver)
- Criterion 6 ✗ 0 ("Meds are okay" isn't a chart location)

**Total: 3 of 12.** Totals aren't the point — the *pattern* is. This student notices people beautifully and hands off structure terribly. Now you know exactly what to drill.

## Reliability check (the pass/fail your rubric must survive)

Before you inflict a rubric on students, give a colleague your criteria *without* telling them which student wrote what, and have them score one recorded (fictional) handoff. If your totals differ by more than 2 points, find the row where you disagreed and sharpen its level-1 description. Repeat once. Two rounds is normally enough.

## Anti-patterns to delete on sight

- "Demonstrates professional demeanor" — cannot be observed, invite your own bias
- Combining unrelated checks in one row ("Timely and accurate") — split them
- Half-points — they multiply grading arguments by five
- Silently weighting safety double — if it matters that much, it deserves its own highlighted row
