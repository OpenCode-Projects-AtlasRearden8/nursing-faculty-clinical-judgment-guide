Ten-minute lessons students teach each other — the format that makes them actually learn the thing.

The big idea

A student who can teach a topic in ten minutes owns it. A student who stumbles on step three has shown you — kindly, harmlessly — exactly where their understanding is hollow, with a patient nowhere near the room. Micro-lessons are the cheapest simulator you own.

This guide gives you the format, the rotation plan, the prep worksheet, and the facilitation moves.

The 10-minute micro-lesson format

Every micro-lesson has the same skeleton. Making the skeleton predictable is what makes it safe.

MinuteSegmentWhat happens
0–1HookA question, a fictional scenario, or a "here's the wrong way" opening
1–4TeachThe core, no slides required. Three facts max. Say which is non-negotiable
4–7ShowDemonstrate on a fictional case, a prop, or a handout — the class can see it, not just hear it
7–9TryEveryone in the room does one rep: a teach-back, a one-sentence answer, a quick sort
9–10Exit checkOne question everyone answers on a card before leaving

Rules for the student-presenter:

  • You get the same skeleton everyone gets. It's not a personality contest — that's the point.
  • Slides are optional; the Try segment is not.
  • If you get stuck, that's teachable: say "here's where I'd look," and model looking it up instead of bluffing.

Choosing topics that fit ten minutes

Good micro-lesson topics are narrow: one rule, one tool, one doorway moment, one assessment trick.

Fits in 10 min ✅Does not fit ❌
The doorway conversation with a family"Communicating with patients"
Reporting one abnormal lab value"Lab interpretations"
What SBAR's S and B are for, specifically"SBAR" (all four)
One handoff phrase and why it works"Handoff best practices"
Reading a wound-care order's timing"Wound care"

The topic test: could you cover it with three short facts and one demonstrable move? If yes, it's a micro-lesson. If it needs a flowchart, split it.

The rotation plan (for a 12-student clinical group)

Chunk the semester into block weeks; lessons land in post-conference.

  • Weeks 1–2: You model the format once (pick something fun). Then two brave volunteers. Nobody grades yet.
  • Weeks 3–6: Two micro-lessons per week, topics assigned by lottery from a topic jar. Presenters submit a completed prep worksheet to you 48 hours ahead — review for safety and scope, not polish.
  • Weeks 7–12: Same cadence, but peers score with a three-item peer rubric (below), and each presenter writes a two-sentence self-debrief afterward.
  • Final week: The class votes for the "clearest of the semester," and you keep all of the exit-check cards — they're your free-term assessment of what landed.

The prep worksheet

Hand this out with the topic. It runs one page.

Topic: ____________________
The one sentence my audience must remember: ______________________
My three facts: 1) ____________  2) ____________  3) ____________
My hook (a question or fictional scenario): ______________________
My show (what I'll demonstrate/display): ______________________
My try (what everyone will DO for two minutes): ______________________
My exit-check question (one, answerable in one sentence): ______________________
Anything I'm unsure about (turn this in early — asking is the skill): __________

The peer rubric (three items, whole numbers)

Keep peer feedback tiny and kind. Presenters stay in the room, which is why the rubric is soft-touched.

Item012
Clarity: I can repeat the core sentence in my own wordsNoAlmostYes, easily
Show: Something appeared on the board / prop / sheetNothingTalked about itShowed it
Try: I did something myself, it wasn't just listeningNot reallyWatched others do itDid it, kept it

Then each peer writes exactly one completive sentence: "Something I'd steal from your lesson: ______" (steal = a compliment with teeth).

Facilitation moves for you, the educator

  • Protect the presenter. If peers pile on: "One growth edge at a time — the Creator gets to pick which feedback lands today."
  • Rescue without taking over. If the lesson stalls in minute five: hand them the hook question again as their lifeline — "Tell them your hook one more time, then walk us through your three facts."
  • Save the failures. A shaky presentation isn't a failure of the format; it's your most honest map of the class's understanding. Debrief it with the class, never the presenter, using the wrong-answer-as-data frame from Guide 1.
  • Credit for the 48-hour early submission. The willingness to show you a draft is the actual clinical-judgment behavior we're training: ask early, escalate early.

Where it connects

  • The exit-check cards give you next week's ladder questions (Guide 2).
  • Recurring exit-check weaknesses become the rubric rows you actually need (Guide 4).
  • Family-handoff micro-lessons are the highest-yield topic jar item (Guide 3).

The 30-second version

Two micro-lessons a week, one filled skeleton, three-item peer rubric, and the presenter's shaky spot is your best teaching map of the whole semester.
